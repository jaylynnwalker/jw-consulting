Platform administration · Power BI / Fabric

Running the whole estate, scaled to fit each team.

A reference for administering Power BI across multiple teams and departments of varying size — the operating model, how it scales, and the concrete conventions to apply. Place any department, from a 3-person team to an enterprise rollout, on a known ladder and govern it consistently.

Prepared by Ramon Carroll, P3 Adaptive · For Jaylynn Walker & Hannah Jensen · 2026-06-03

1 The operating model

"Discipline at the core, flexibility at the edge."

Microsoft defines three content-ownership strategies. The key insight: a healthy organization runs all three at once — different departments sit at different points depending on size, skill, and how critical their content is.

StrategyWho owns whatGovernanceFits
Business-led self-serviceBusiness unit owns everything (data + reports)LightestPersonal / small-team, exploration
Managed self-servicerecommendedCentral team owns data & models; BU owns reportsModerateTeam & departmental BI
EnterpriseCentral team owns end-to-endMost rigorousMission-critical, broad distribution
Recommended default for KurarayManaged self-service. You own and certify the shared semantic models (one source of truth); business units build their own thin reports. It's the shape GoPlant already takes — you own the model, consumers get reports — so you're generalizing a pattern you've started, not inventing one.

The governance dial = ownership scope × delivery breadth × criticality. A report one analyst uses needs almost no governance; a model feeding the whole sales org's commission numbers needs a lot. Govern each asset to its stakes — not to a single org-wide rule. MS: content ownership & management

2 The maturity ladder

Microsoft's adoption roadmap aligns to a 5-level Capability Maturity Model. Use it as a worksheet: rate each department and track them individually — business units mature at different rates. Target level 300–400 where it matters; don't chase 500 everywhere.

3 Target architecture by department size

The same principles apply at every size — separate model from report, least privilege, one source of truth. What changes is how much machinery wraps them. Scale the ceremony to the stakes.

Small department

A few creators, a handful of reports.

  • 2 workspaces — data + reporting
  • Promoted endorsement
  • dev→prod (manual ok)
  • App distribution; ungoverned level

Large department

Many creators, many models/reports, mixed maturity.

  • Multiple data + reporting workspaces
  • Certified canonical models
  • dev/test/prod pipelines, separate for model vs report
  • Security-group roles; Git on .pbip; governed

Enterprise-wide

Many departments, mixed ownership.

  • Fabric domains + delegated admin
  • Tenant-settings strategy per security group
  • Capacity governance + chargeback
  • A Center of Excellence function
The dividing lineThe line between these rungs is mostly licensing (§6). Pipelines, Fabric Git, domains, certification workflows, and free-viewer distribution are all capacity-gated. Know the tier before promising the architecture.

4 Workspaces & roles

Separate data workspaces from reporting workspaces. The foundational move — it decouples data from analytics, limits who can edit the critical/certified models, keeps RLS/OLS enforced for builders and viewers, and removes co-ownership ambiguity.

DATA workspace  ──live connect──▶  REPORTING workspace  ──App──▶  Viewers
(shared model,                     (thin reports + App,            (read-only)
 you own)                           business unit owns)

Roles map directly to least privilege — assign security groups, not people

RoleCanBuild on models?Use for
AdminEverything incl. manage access, deleteyesThe platform team (you)
MemberEdit, publish apps, add othersyesTrusted senior creators
ContributorCreate / edit contentyesReport / model developers
ViewerRead onlynoConsumers entering the workspace
Build ≠ securityBuild permission gates who can author on a shared model — but Read alone still exposes the data. Build is not a substitute for RLS/OLS. MS: build permission

Two governance levels, kept simple: governed vs ungoverned. Governed workspaces carry requirements (domain assignment, certified content, dev/test/prod, security-group roles, active auditing). Classify by four criteria: ownership, delivery scope, data subject area, criticality. Deploy in one direction only (dev→test→prod); never hand-publish from a desktop straight to prod. 5 Domains — organizing at scale (Fabric)

Once you have many workspaces, Fabric domains keep it sane. A domain groups all the workspaces for a business area (Sales, Finance, HR…) into a single management boundary — a data-mesh pattern: distributed ownership, central/federated governance.

  • A workspace belongs to exactly one domain; items inherit it. Domains also drive discovery via the OneLake catalog.
  • Organize four ways — by subject area (Finance), owning team (Enterprise BI), business unit/segment (a regional division), or project. Pick how the org thinks.
  • Assign workspaces by name (convention), by admin (security group), or by capacity.

Three-tier delegated administration

Fabric admin (you)

Create/edit/delete domains; appoint domain admins & contributors.

Domain admin = business owner

Manages only their domain; can override delegated tenant settings. Can't delete/rename or change other admins.

Domain contributor

Workspace admin authorized to assign their own workspaces into the domain.

Why it matters for a 2-person teamThis is how you hand day-to-day control to business owners without losing central governance — "federated governance." Requires Fabric capacity; the delegable-settings list is limited and partly preview — verify against live docs. MS: Fabric domains

7 The operating function — a "virtual CoE"

You don't need a formal Center of Excellence to do CoE work. Microsoft is explicit: a chartered CoE is recommended but not required, and it's common for a centralized data team to absorb CoE responsibilities — that's you. Start centralized; evolve to federated (delegating to domain owners) as the estate grows.

Govern iteratively and lightly — Microsoft names iterative governance "the best choice for most organizations," and says use the most lightweight model that meets the objective. For a 2-person team, that's permission to not over-engineer.

Minimal operating cadence judgmentWeekly: triage new content & refresh failures; onboard requests.  Monthly: review usage metrics & activity log; review certification requests.  Quarterly: tenant-settings review; re-rate each department on the ladder; capacity health.  Always-on: a published naming convention, a data-ownership policy, and a "definition of certified."

8 Playbook

8a · Naming convention judgment

Adapt freely — consistency matters more than the exact scheme.

Workspace:    KUR-<Dept>-<Data|Rpt>-<Dev|Prod>    e.g.  KUR-Sales-Data-Prod
Domain:       <Business Area>                       e.g.  Sales
Semantic model: <Subject> Model                     e.g.  Sales Performance Model
App:          <Dept> <Audience>                      e.g.  Sales Leadership
Security grp: SG-PBI-<Dept>-<Role>                   e.g.  SG-PBI-Sales-Viewers

Decide naming, workspace-creation rights, domains, and governance levels at the tenant level, early — they affect everything downstream.

8b · Controlling workspace creation at scale

Don't let anyone create workspaces, and don't fully centralize it. Create a security group (e.g., "Fabric Workspace Creators") of trained central and trusted local users, and set the "Create workspaces" tenant setting to it. Scope tenant settings to security groups, not the whole org — capable teams get more flexibility within your guardrails. MS: tenant-level workspace planning

8c · Taking over the orphaned sales reports the immediate job

Microsoft prescribes a formal ownership transfer — and the new owner refactors and validates before taking full ownership (especially the hidden data-prep, modeling, and calculations). Click items to check them off:

  • Inventory & reassign access — find every report/model/dataflow/gateway the departed user owned; move dataset & gateway ownership to the team / a service principal (not another person, to avoid the next bus-factor gap).
  • Refactor & validate — reverse-engineer undocumented Power Query, RLS/OLS, and DAX; confirm the numbers before putting your name on it.
  • Re-platform — lift into separated data/reporting workspaces; publish the model; convert reports to thin (live-connect) reports.
  • Preserve the model interface — renaming tables/columns/measures/hierarchies, changing a column's data type, changing a measure's return type, or moving a measure's home table all break dependent visuals. Refactor the inside; keep the surface stable.
  • Endorse & document — Promote (later Certify) the model; write the data dictionary; assign an owner.
  • Close the gap — add a data-ownership policy (when an asset needs an owner + the owner's duties) so turnover can't orphan content again.

8d · Monitoring & oversight tooling

Admin portal

Tenant settings, capacity, org-wide controls.

Usage metrics

Per-report consumption — find unused content & top consumers.

Activity log / audit

Who did what; build a tenant-wide audit history.

Capacity Metrics app

Capacity health, throttling, hotspots.

Chargeback app

Attribute capacity cost per department — showback drives optimization & funding decisions.

9 Recommended sequence for Kuraray

  • 1 · Confirm the licensing tier (Pro / PPU / Fabric + consumer counts) — gates everything below.
  • 2 · Pilot managed self-service on Sales — separated workspaces, a shared model, thin reports, an App, dev→prod.
  • 3 · Take over & stabilize the orphaned reports (§8c); assign owners; write the data-ownership policy.
  • 4 · Publish the standards (naming, roles, governance levels, "definition of certified") and the maturity ladder; rate each department.
  • 5 · Add scale machinery as justified — pipelines/Git at PPU/capacity; domains + delegated admin + chargeback at Fabric.
The one thing to rememberOwn the core. Free the edge. Scale the ceremony to the stakes. One operating model — managed self-service — applied at the right intensity for each department's size and criticality.
